All content is `candidate`; nothing is verified. Items marked **[CHECK]** are where a native-speaker or linguist reviewer should look first.

How this was checked: the four realizations, the English locale additions and the bridge were overlaid on a scratch copy of the live `gef-lexicon` tree (`.../gen4/id_work/stage/`, rebuilt by `restage.sh`; it removes any live `id` files first and overlays the drop with `stage_merge.py`), and `scripts/validate-curriculum-v2.mjs` passed with `id` as `completeness: "complete"`, `slots_version: 2`, with no label or gloss conflicts against the live locale. ## A. Things that need a decision (read these first)

1. **[CHECK] `trait.classifiers: required` overstates.** The generic label says "you can’t put a number straight in front of a noun: you add a counting word". Indonesian counting words (orang for people, ekor for animals, buah for things) are standard, and `dua orang guru`, `tiga ekor kucing`, `lima buah buku` are the careful forms, but a bare `dua buku` is also heard, and Wikipedia says they are "not necessary just to say a". `required` is the closest value (`none` would say "counts the way you do", which is wrong for people and animals). A reviewer may want a third value (`common`) or a softer label.
2. **[CHECK] `trait.word_building: isolating`** (the brief's "analytic"). Indonesian has no inflection (no tense, person, number or case endings); tense words (sudah, akan) and reduplication (buku-buku) do that work, so the `isolating` label ("words hardly change: small separate words and word order do the work that endings do elsewhere") is true. But it also has a very productive set of derivational affixes (me-, ber-, di-, per-, ke-, -kan, -an, -i; baca, membaca, pembaca, bacaan). `agglutinating` was the alternative, but its label ("one long word can be a whole sentence") is not Indonesian. The affixes are shown as the fourth example, not claimed in the label. A slot for "derivational affixes" would be exact.
3. **[CHECK] `trait.formal_you: distinct`** (informal `kamu`, formal `Anda`). In real use the picture is richer: `Anda` is polite but can sound distant (ads, forms, strangers), people often address each other with kin or status words (Bapak, Ibu, Kakak, Mas, Mbak) or drop the pronoun, and `kau` / `engkau` are the intimate forms. `levels` would say "built into its verbs and words", which is wrong (Indonesian verbs have no politeness levels), so `distinct` is closest. Examples: `Siapa namamu?` (informal, the clitic -mu is "your") against `Siapa nama Anda?` (formal).
4. **[CHECK] `trait.verb_endings: none`.** True of person, number and tense. The label "verbs never change their form" is slightly loose: the active prefix me- (with nasal changes), the passive di- and the suffixes -kan and -i change a verb's role. They are shown in the word-building example, not in this trait.
5. **[CHECK] `trait.questions: intonation_or_inversion`** with the optional particle `apakah` as a qualifier (as the Swahili file does for `je`). A yes/no question is usually just a statement with rising intonation (`Kamu lapar?`); `apakah` (or the suffix -kah) is the formal marker. `particle` is defensible (the Japanese file chose it for か); I chose the one that matches everyday speech. Both are shown, the second with `particle: apakah`.
6. **[CHECK] `trait.subject_pronouns: context_dependent`.** Pronouns are often left out when the context is clear (`Sudah makan?`, `Mau ke mana?`) and nothing on the verb recovers them, so `often_dropped` (whose label says "because the verb ending already says who") would be wrong.
7. **Slots the contract does not have, so this data cannot carry them** (suggestions): topic-comment sentences and no copula (`Ibu saya guru`, "My mother, teacher": no "is"); the clitic possessives -ku, -mu, -nya and the passive prefix di-; inclusive and exclusive "we" (kita, kami); reduplication as a plural (only shown as an example of `word_building`); the e/pepet contrast has no slot (only the tricky-sound fact `e` and the mark ê).
8. **[CHECK] Three letters whose tile sound is not the letter you expect.** `v` is `sound: f`, `x` is `sound: s`, `q` is `sound: k`. The reasons: Indonesian speakers "virtually always" say [f] for v (Wiktionary appendix; EYD V gives the pronunciation of the letter name as "fe"); a word-initial x is said [s] (EYD V: xenon, xilofon) and is [ks] only in the middle or at the end of a word (no everyday word has one); q is /k/ (Wiktionary letter entry; EYD V: name "qi", said "ki"). The tile will read "/f/", "/s/", "/k/". A short caption such as "in Indonesian, said like f" would help. Note that `v`, `x` and `q` are loan letters (EYD V: only for names and special fields); `f` and `z` are loan letters too, and the sounds `ʃ` (sy) and `x` (kh) are loan sounds, but all of these are common in everyday words.
9. **[CHECK] Final k is written `ʔ` in word IPA** (itik `ˈitiʔ`, tidak `ˈtidaʔ`, amuk, batik). The brief said "k at word end = glottal stop"; Wikipedia ("Help:IPA/Indonesian": final k [ʔ]) and Wiktionary's pronunciation appendix ("a glottal stop [ʔ] or an unreleased plosive") agree, but Wiktionary's own word entries write `/k/` and `[k̚]` (`/ˈti.dak/`). I wrote `ʔ` only for a word-final k; a syllable-final k in the middle of a word is plain `k` (kakaktua `kakakˈtua`). `sounds.consonants` lists `ʔ`, so the "new sound" comparison flags it for an English reader.
10. **[CHECK] The example for `q` is `alquran`.** There is no everyday lowercase word with q. KBBI's standard spelling since 2019 is `Al-Qur'an` (a name, capital Q, which the app cannot match to the lowercase symbol `q`); `alquran` was the earlier KBBI headword and is still common. I used it because the app finds a symbol's example by its lowercase `highlight`. Alternatives: `qurban` (written with q on banners, KBBI standard `kurban`), `taqwa` (KBBI `takwa`), `qari`. Its IPA `alˈkuran` follows the letter entry (q is /k/); Wiktionary's rule-based template gives `/alˈʔu.ran/` (q as a glottal stop).
11. **[CHECK] One branch id.** `branch_ids: ["malayic"]`, not `malayo_polynesian, malayic` as the Russian file chains `slavic, east_slavic`. The app's relation check treats any shared branch id as "same branch", so a chain through Malayo-Polynesian would make Javanese and Filipino read as "close cousins" of Indonesian. With `malayic` alone, Malay (`ms`) reads as a close cousin and Javanese and Filipino as "distant cousins". The scene reads "Indonesian belongs to the Austronesian family, in the Malayic branch". If another author gives Javanese or Filipino a `malayo_polynesian` branch, the label `branch.malayo_polynesian` is not written here.
12. **`reach.regions: ["asia"]`** (and `origin_region_ids`). By the UN geoscheme Indonesia is entirely in South-eastern Asia; the Papua provinces are on New Guinea, which is Oceania in other schemes. Add `oceania` if you prefer. Indonesian is also a recognised working language in Timor-Leste (Wikipedia) and spoken by a diaspora in the Netherlands, not listed.

## C. Conventions I chose (please confirm or change)

**The alphabet**
- One layer, `latin`, `alphabet`, `ltr`, `has_case: true`, `joined: false`, `vowels: all_written`, role `primary`.
- `symbols` are the 26 letters a to z, lowercase, in alphabet order. The digraphs `ng ny sy kh` (each "one sound" in PUEBI) and the diphthongs `ai au ei oi` are spelled from letters the learner has, so they are explained in the sound stop, not listed.
- **Six teaching groups of 4 or 5, easy first for an English reader:** (1) `b d p t s` and (2) `m n l w f`: consonants that look and sound like English (p, t unaspirated is taught in the sound stop); (3) `a i u o`: the four plain vowels, said as in Spanish or Italian; (4) `c e j y`: the letters that surprise other readers (c is "ch", e is mostly "uh", j is "j" and y is "y", which a German or Spanish reader does not expect); (5) `g h k r`: g is always hard, h is a light breath, k at the end of a word is a throat stop, r is a tap; (6) `v z x q`: the loan letters, last. Every symbol is in exactly one group, and **no example word contains another letter of its own group**, so the app's "which letter is in the word you hear?" and "match each letter to a word" questions never have two right answers. **[CHECK]** `e` is deliberately not in the vowel group: its natural example `emas` contains `a`, and the words of a group may not contain each other's letters; `e` is also the vowel with the false-friend sound, so it sits with `c j y`.
- `name` is the letter's Indonesian name (PUEBI 2015 and EYD V 2022): a, be, ce, de, e, ef, ge, ha, i, je, ka, el, em, en, o, pe, ki, er, es, te, u, ve, we, eks, ye, zet. All 26 are distinct. **[CHECK]** EYD V writes the name of `q` as "qi" (said "ki") and PUEBI 2015 as "ki"; I wrote `ki`. EYD V gives `v` the name "ve" but the pronunciation "fe".
- `sound` is the letter's own main sound, broad IPA: `c tʃ`, `e ə` (the most frequent value of e; see the tricky fact), `g ɡ` (U+0261), `j dʒ`, `q k`, `v f`, `x s`, `y j`, `r r`, the rest as expected. There is no `reading` (the script is Latin). There is no `looks_like` (every symbol is a Latin letter the learner already reads).
- **Punctuation: the hyphen.** `punctuation: ["-"]`, example `ibu-ibu` ("women, ladies": ibu, "mother" or "ma'am", said twice), name `tanda hubung (hyphen)`, no `sound` (as the Russian and Japanese files). In Indonesian the hyphen is how reduplication, the usual plural (buku-buku, "books"), is written, which is the one punctuation fact an English reader may not know. **[CHECK]** keep or drop; nothing depends on it. (`ibu-ibu` is 6 letters.) For an English reader the short script lesson is therefore the hyphen plus the mark ê (every letter is already known).
- **Mark: circumflex ê** (role `vowel_quality`), `writing.latin.mark.circumflex`, example `sêri` (`səˈri`, "a tie, a draw"; `seri` with a clear e is "a series"). EYD V 2022 says the optional diacritic ê may be written on a pepet e to tell words apart, with the examples têras and sêri. It is optional and rare. Earlier editions (2009, 2015) also allowed é and è; EYD V mentions only ê, so only ê is a mark fact. `writing.latin.marked` lists `ê` (no tile of its own, as with the Russian stress vowels). **[CHECK]** that a text-to-speech voice may ignore or mispronounce the ê in `sêri`.
- **Example words** (one per letter, 2 to 7 letters, no word used twice, native where a native word is as common, each word shown in the IPA that Wiktionary gives, simplified below): ayam, bola, cincin, daun, emas, foto, gigi, hujan, itik, jam, kuda, laut, mata, nasi, otot, pohon, alquran, roti, susu, tangan, ungu, visa, wajah, xilofon, payung, zaitun. Loans where no native word starts the letter: `foto`, `visa`, `xilofon`, `zaitun`, `alquran` (v, x, z, q, f are loan letters). **[CHECK]** that none reads oddly: `itik` ("duck", `bebek` is the everyday word in Java), `otot` ("muscle"), `ungu` ("purple"), `emas` ("gold"; it has the weak e, stress on the last syllable), `payung` (the y is in the middle; no everyday native word starts with y except `yang`, "which", and `yakin`, "sure"), `cincin` ("ring"), `laut` ("sea"), `visa` (said "fisa").
- **Words not chosen, and why.** `ember` for e (bucket): I could not confirm that its e is a pepet. `video` and `zebra` (for v and z): Wiktionary's rule gives them a schwa (`/vi.dəˈo/`, `/zəbˈra/`), which looks wrong for a loan word that is usually said with a clear e, so I avoided every example whose e is uncertain. `yoga`/`yoyo` for y (loans). `kucing` and `kopi` for k (contain letters of its group).

**Sounds**
- IPA is broad phonemic, from Wiktionary's Indonesian pronunciation (`{{id-IPA}}`), simplified as follows: tie bars removed (`tʃ dʒ`); no syllable dots; `ˈ` on the stressed syllable of every word of two or more syllables, with the schwa rule applied (stress moves off a schwa in the penultimate syllable: `əˈmas`, `səˈri`); no secondary stress except where Wiktionary shows it (`səˌlamat ˈpaɡi`, `təˌrima ˈkasih`, `ˌoraŋ ˈutan`); the lax vowels [ɪ ʊ ɛ ɔ] that Wiktionary shows in closed final syllables are written as the tense vowels (`itiʔ`, `ˈotot`, `ˈhotel` for Wiktionary's `/ˈhotɛl/`); final k as `ʔ` (see A9); `v` as `f` (A8); dental `t̪` and unreleased `p̚ t̚ k̚` not written.
- `sounds.consonants`: `p b t d k ɡ ʔ tʃ dʒ f s z ʃ x h m n ɲ ŋ r l j w` (23). `f z ʃ x` occur in loan words only but are everyday; `v` is left out (said as f); `q` has no sound of its own. `sounds.vowels`: `a i u e ə o` (6; the lax allophones and the diphthongs `ai au oi ei` are not listed).
- **Eight `sounds.tricky.*`**, no tricky word containing another tricky spelling (the app's "which spelling makes the sound in this word?" question offers all the spellings as options): `c` (cabai), `ng` (bunga), `ny` (punya, `sound.palatal_nasal` reused), `sy` (syal), `kh` (khusus, `sound.velar_fricative` reused), final k (tidak, spelling written `-k` so it is not a substring of `kh`), `r` (roti, `sound.tapped_r` reused), `e` (emas). `j` is not a tricky sound for an English reader (it is the "j" of "jam"); it is covered by the contrast pair cari/jari and the letter groups. The unaspirated p, t, k is not a tricky fact (every word has one, so the spelling question would have no clean answer); it is mentioned in `sound.id_stops`.
- **Four contrast pairs**, all real, common words: cari/jari ("to look for" / "finger"; c is "ch", j is "j"), dua/tua ("two" / "old"; d and t), lupa/rupa ("to forget" / "appearance, form"; l and r), mata/mati ("eye" / "dead, to die"; the last vowel). No pair is given for the final k (bapa and bapak are the same word with and without the stop) or for the two sounds of e (the spelling does not show which one a word has).
- `stress.pattern: mostly_penultimate`: the registry value; the generic label says "second-to-last syllable". Wiktionary and Wikipedia: stress is usually penultimate, shifting to the last syllable of a two-syllable word whose first vowel is a schwa. Some researchers describe Indonesian as having no true word stress (Wikipedia, Malay phonology). `tones.system: none`.
- `spelling.predictability: high` **[CHECK]**. Letters map to sounds very regularly; the exceptions are the two sounds of `e` (written alike), the loan letters (v, x, q) and a few Arabic loans (kh, sy). `medium` is defensible.

**Grammar** (see section A for the imperfect fits). `noun_gender: none`, `dual_number: none`, `cases: none`, `articles: none` (ini and itu can do the work of "the" but are demonstratives), `adjective_position: after_noun` (rumah besar, buku baru, kucing hitam), `word_order: svo` (Saya makan nasi; Ani membaca buku). No `noun.*` facts (no gender). Every `trait.*.example.*` has a gloss in the locale.

**Overview**
- `family_id: austronesian`, `branch_ids: [malayic]`, `related_language_tags: [ms, jv, fil]` (all in the registry; Malay is the close one, Javanese and Filipino distant), `script_ids: [Latn]`, `origin_region_ids: [asia]`. I did not add `ancestor_language_tags` (Old Malay, ISO 639-3 `omy`); add it if the app resolves ancestors.
- Hook: `hook.target` `selamat pagi` ("good morning", a very common greeting; two words), decoys `terima kasih` ("thank you") and `air` ("water", which an English reader will take for "air": a small trap and the same word as a false friend in the bridge). **[CHECK]** `halo` ("hello", a loan from English) was left out as too easy; `apa kabar` ("how are you") is the alternative if a one-word target is preferred.

## D. Bridge (`bridge-en-id.json`)

- English and Indonesian are not related, so there is **one `helpful_transfer` note** (the app shows only the first note's text as the page text), whose text says honestly which kind each group is: first English words that came from Malay or Javanese (12), then Indonesian words borrowed from Dutch or English (8). 20 pairs.
- **English words from Malay or Indonesian, verified in en.wiktionary etymology sections:** orangutan (probably via Dutch, from Malay `orang hutan` / `orang utan`, "forest person"), amok (from Portuguese `amouco`, from Malay `amuk`), bamboo (Malay `bambu`, by way of Portuguese and Dutch), gong (Malay `gong`, possibly imitative), sarong (Malay `sarung`), rattan (Malay `rotan`), ketchup (the route is uncertain: Hokkien `kê-chiap`, "fish sauce", possibly through Malay `kecap` or `kicap`; Wiktionary marks the Malay step as uncertain, and the note text says "may have come this way too"), cockatoo (Dutch `kaketoe`, from Malay `kakaktua`), paddy (Malay `padi`), batik (Javanese `bathik`; the note text says "or from Javanese"), durian (Malay `durian`), satay (Malay `sate`; ultimately Tamil). **[CHECK]** the Indonesian forms: `kakaktua` is the standard spelling (Wiktionary marks `kakatua` as proscribed); `orang utan` is two words (Wiktionary's Indonesian headword; `orang hutan` is a variant; the KBBI III mirror lists neither as a headword, KBBI V was not reachable); `kecap` is also the word for a lip smack in KBBI (the soy sauce is `kécap` /ˈketʃap/ in Wiktionary); `sate` has the variant `satai` (KBBI /saté/).
- **Left out:** gecko (the source is unclear: Dutch `gekko`, perhaps Malay `gekoq`, perhaps Acehnese), compound (an enclosure; only "possibly" from Malay `kampung`, with the Latin verb mixed in), sago (Malay `sagu`, fine, dropped only for length), cassowary, dugong, mangosteen, lahar, tempeh (all fine candidates).
- **Words Indonesian borrowed (8):** computer/komputer (from English), taxi/taksi, bus/bus, police/polisi, hotel/hotel, restaurant/restoran, telephone/telepon, station/stasiun (all from Dutch, in Wiktionary's etymologies; the Dutch words themselves come through French and Latin or Greek, as the English ones do). The brief's `kantor` (Dutch `kantoor`, English "office") and `handuk` (Dutch `handdoek`, English "towel") were dropped: they are Dutch borrowings with no English look-alike. `to_ipa` follows the realization convention (`ˈhotel` for Wiktionary's `/ˈhotɛl/`).
- **One `false_friend` note, 6 pairs** (all meanings checked in the KBBI mirror): air (water), jam (hour, clock), sore (late afternoon; KBBI /soré/), cat (paint; said "chat"), ban (tyre), rok (skirt, set against English "rock"). **[CHECK]** `rok` is a looser false friend (the spelling differs); and with `sore`, `cat` and `ban` a native speaker may know more common ones.
