# German (`de`): needs, conventions and items to check

All content is `candidate`, nothing is verified. Items marked **[CHECK]** are where a native-speaker or linguist reviewer should look first.

How this was checked: the four realizations, the English locale additions and the bridge were overlaid on a scratch copy of the current `gef-lexicon` (fresh copy of `curriculum-v2/`, `schemas/`, `scripts/` taken after the em-dash rule landed), and `scripts/validate-curriculum-v2.mjs` passed with `de` as `completeness: "complete"` and `slots_version: 2` (all other complete languages still pass beside it). Removing a gloss and a label in the copy made it fail, so the check does reach `de`. The real tree was not touched. The generator is in `.../gen4/de_work/` (`build.py`, `data_writing.py`, `fmt.py`, `merge.py`); edit the data there and re-run `python3 build.py`. No file contains U+2014 (checked), and no `\u` escapes.

Sources: en.wiktionary German entries (the `{{de-IPA}}` output, read through the MediaWiki parse API) for every word's IPA, simplified as described below; en.wikipedia "German orthography" (30 letters counted as 26 + ä ö ü ß, letter names, c q v w z j s y ß pronunciations, ẞ made official in 2017 but SS remains standard in capitals, ch rule, sp/st, quotation marks „ “); en.wikipedia "German phonology" (consonant and vowel inventory, ich-Laut / ach-Laut, final devoicing, r realizations, stress, the disputed /ɛː/); en.wikipedia "German language" (regions, West Germanic, ISO code `goh`).

## 2. Conventions I chose (please confirm or change)

**Letters and groups**
- `symbols` are 30: a to z, then `ä ö ü ß` (the school alphabet; Wikipedia says 26 to 30 depending on how the four are counted). `marked` is `ä ö ü` as the brief asked, so for a reader who already has `ü` as a marked letter (Spanish) the lesson skips it.
- **`ß` is lowercase only** (it has no standard capital; the optional capital ẞ, U+1E9E, has been allowed since 2017 but SS is still the norm). `has_case` is `true` for the layer, so the lesson tile builds its glyph with `toLocaleUpperCase`, which turns ß into "SS" and shows "SS ß". That is honest but may look odd. **[CHECK]** whether the app should special-case ß (show only "ß"). German has no lowercase-only note label; if wanted, the sentence above could go there.
- **Eight teaching groups of 3 to 5, easy first, every symbol once**: (1) b d f k and (2) g l m n and (3) p t h, the consonants that look and sound like English ones; (4) a e i o u, the pure vowels; (5) j v w z, the false friends (ja is "yah", v says f, w says v, z says ts); (6) r s y, three more surprises (the throaty r, s before a vowel says z, y says ü); (7) ä ö ü ß; (8) c q x, the rare ones (loanwords and "qu"). **[CHECK]** the vowel group's place and the idea of putting `h` with `p t`.
- **No example word in a group contains another letter of the same group in any case** (so the app's "which letter is in the word you hear?" never has two right answers). It was checked by script for all eight groups, including capitals (Löwe contains L).
- **Examples show the lowercase letter as written.** German capitalises nouns, but the lesson looks an example up by `highlight === symbol` (the lowercase symbol) and tints the word with a case-sensitive split on that symbol, so a capital initial (Apfel, Ball) would give no tint. So where a letter sits first in a word I used a lowercase-initial word (adjectives, numbers, adverbs, verbs: alt, blau, cool, drei, fünf, gut, heiß, klein, lila, neun, quer, rot, rund, sieben, trinken, vier, warm, zehn, ja, du, wir), and where a noun is the best picture the letter sits inside it in lowercase (Lampe, Taxi, Typ, Käse, Löwe, Tür, Fuß, müde). Consequently not every example starts with its letter (e, i, o, p, u, x, y, ä ö ü ß do not). **[CHECK]** whether the lesson should instead tint case-insensitively; then plainer picture nouns (Apfel, Ball, Haus, Esel, Igel, Hund, Zahn) would be better and are listed in section 6.
- Words are all 7 letters or fewer, concrete where a lowercase-initial word allows it. Loanwords: `cool` (c), `Taxi` (x), `lila` (l); there is no native German word with a standalone c, so any example for c is a loanword. `Typ` for y is Greek-derived (y says ü there); the English loanwords with y (Handy, Baby, Hobby) say i. **[CHECK]** `cool` for c, `quer` for q ("across, sideways", an adjective; q only ever appears in qu, and nearly all common qu-words are capitalised nouns), `Typ` for y, `du` and `wir` (pronouns, but with a lowercase long vowel).
- `heiß`, `fünf`, `müde` and `Tür` contain ß or ü before those letters are taught. They only tint the letter they teach, so it is harmless, but a full lesson for a non-Latin reader will play them early.
- **`sound` for vowels is the long vowel** (aː is written `a`, since long and short a differ only in length): `a eː iː oː uː`, `ä ɛː`, `ö øː`, `ü yː`, `y yː`, so the tile says what the letter says when it says its name, and the example word has that vowel. Short vowels are taught by the long/short contrast pair (Beet / Bett) and its label, not by a tile. **[CHECK]** `ä` as `ɛː` (the long ä is a disputed phoneme: Wikipedia says it often merges with eː; many speakers say `eː` in Käse).
- `c` is `k` (loanwords, Computer, Café; before e i ä ö it says ts, Cent, as the letter's name Ce does). `q` is `kv` because q only occurs as qu; the tile will read "/kv/". `s` is `z` (before a vowel, as in sieben; in the middle or at the end before a consonant it is s; at the start before p or t it is ʃ). `v` is `f` (Vater, vier; a few loans say v: Vase). `x` is `ks`. **[CHECK]** all of these as "the letter's own typical sound".
- **`name` carries an English respelling in brackets**, in the style the Greek file uses ("βήτα (vita)"): "Be (bay)", "Ce (tsay)", "Jot (yot)", "Vau (fow)", "We (vay)", "Ix (iks)", "Zett (tset)", "Ypsilon (ÜP-si-lon)", "Eszett (ess-tset)", "Ä (eh)", "Ö (öh)", "Ü (üh)". The letter names (aː, beː, tseː, deː, eː, ɛf, ɡeː, haː, iː, jɔt, kaː, ɛl, ɛm, ɛn, oː, peː, kuː, ɛʁ, ɛs, teː, uː, faʊ̯, veː, ɪks, ˈʏpsilɔn, tsɛt) are from en.wikipedia "German orthography" and German Wiktionary conventions. **[CHECK]** the respellings; if the tile should show the plain name, drop the bracket.
- **No `reading` on any Latin letter.** The validator requires it only for non-Latin scripts, and a respelling "b · /b/" would make the match and sound drills trivial. Without `reading` the lesson falls back to its name, word and "match a word" questions, which suit a Latin alphabet. The tile then shows `/IPA/` only. **[CHECK]** whether English-friendly respellings (j "y", v "f", w "v", z "ts") on the false friends would be worth having.
- **`looks_like`** is set on `j` (J), `v` (V), `w` (W), `z` (Z), because an English reader will say them as English letters, and on `ß` (B), `ä` (a), `ö` (o), `ü` (u). **[CHECK]** the oddity: on the j tile the app prints "Looks like J" (it is the same letter, the warning is that the sound differs). A label such as "Looks like {letter}, sounds different" would read better. `s c r y` were not marked (they too differ from English but not as sharply).
- **Short lesson for Latin readers.** The app makes the lesson short when a base already reads the script. An English reader will therefore see only ä ö ü ß, „ and the umlaut mark; a Spanish reader ä ö ß, „ and the mark (ü is in Spanish `marked`; Spanish also has `diaeresis`, so the mark card is skipped for them). The full eight groups are used for readers of non-Latin scripts. The false friends j v w z are therefore carried, for English readers, by the sound stop (tricky `w`, `z`) and by `looks_like`, not by a lesson card. **[CHECK]** whether a later "look-alikes" scene should list the `looks_like` letters.

**Punctuation**
- `punctuation` is only `„` (the low opening quotation mark; closing is “, which an English reader already has), with the example `„Danke!“` and `name` "Anführungszeichen unten". No `sound` or `reading`, as the validator allows for punctuation. **[CHECK]** the name; `»…«` (book style) and the Swiss `«…»` are not listed. No dash is listed (the hyphen and dash behave as in English; the house rule bans the em dash in data).

**Marks**
- One mark: `diaeresis` with role `vowel_quality`, value `ä`, example `Väter` (fathers, from Vater), `example_ipa` `ˈfɛːtɐ`. The registry has no `umlaut` id, and the German mark is the same two dots; **[CHECK]** whether `umlaut` should be a separate id (its own label "umlaut (two dots)"): a Spanish reader's diaeresis (`pronounce_vowel`) is a different job from the German umlaut (`vowel_quality`), and the lesson's "marks you already know" check would hide it. The existing label `mark.diaeresis` ("diaeresis (two dots)") is true as far as the dots go.

**Sounds**
- IPA is broad phonemic, adapted from Wiktionary's `{{de-IPA}}` (first `/.../` form): r is written `ʁ` before a vowel and `ɐ̯` after a long vowel (Tür `tyːɐ̯`, wir `viːɐ̯`) or `ɐ` in -er endings (Vater `ˈfaːtɐ`); tie bars removed (`ts`, `pf`); `ɡ` is U+0261; the diphthongs are `aɪ̯ aʊ̯ ɔʏ̯` (Wiktionary's symbol); syllabic consonants written with ə (sieben `ˈziːbən`); the optional glottal stop and aspiration are left out. Every IPA string for a word in the files was compared with Wiktionary by script; the only differences are the r rewrites above, ja (Wiktionary `jäː`), and words Wiktionary gives only in brackets or not at all (Danke, Guten Tag, kühl, Wanderlust, written from the same rules). **[CHECK]** those four plus `Guten Tag`, and that a phonemic transcription with ʁ suits the app's text-to-speech comparison.
- `sounds.consonants` (variety `standard_german`): p b t d k ɡ pf ts tʃ f v s z ʃ ʒ ç x h m n ŋ l ʁ j. **[CHECK]** `ʒ` and `tʃ` (marginal, loans and Deutsch). `sounds.vowels`: iː ɪ yː ʏ eː ɛ ɛː øː œ aː a oː ɔ uː ʊ ə ɐ aɪ̯ aʊ̯ ɔʏ̯. **[CHECK]** `ɛː` (disputed) and `ə ɐ` (unstressed only).
- Ten `sounds.tricky.*`: ich-Laut (`ich`, ç, `sound.de_ich_laut`), ach-Laut (`Buch`, x, `sound.velar_fricative` reused), ü (`kühl`, `sound.front_rounded_vowel` reused), ö (`Öl`, `sound.de_o_umlaut`), r (`rot`, `sound.uvular_r` reused), w says v (`Wolf`; the label also tells v = f), z says ts (`Zahn`), final devoicing (`Tag`, spelling g, k), st at the start of a word (`Stuhl`, spelling st, ipa ʃ; the label also covers sp and sch), pf (`Apfel`). The two ch items share the spelling `ch` on purpose; the app's spelling question keeps one entry per spelling (the later, Buch).
- **The app's "which spelling makes the sound in this word?" offers every tricky spelling as an option**, so no tricky word may contain another tricky spelling. Checked by script: the nine distinct spellings are ch g pf r st w z ö ü, and each of the ten words contains exactly one of them (hence kühl, not Tür, for ü; Öl, not schön; Wolf, not Wasser). Final devoicing uses the spelling `g` for the same reason.
- Four contrast pairs: Beet / Bett (long or short e), können / kennen (ö or e), Tür / Tier (ü or ie), Reise / Riese (ei or ie). Real, common words. **[CHECK]** that a text-to-speech voice says each pair differently.
- **`stress.pattern: unpredictable` [CHECK, imperfect fit].** German stress is mostly on the root, usually the first syllable of a native word, with unstressed prefixes (be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-) and loanwords that keep their own pattern (Elefant, Hotel). None of the existing values says that: `fixed_initial` ("always the first syllable") is false, `mostly_penultimate` is wrong, and `unpredictable` ("learn it with each word") is the safest but too gloomy. Suggest a new value `mostly_initial` with a label such as "Most words are stressed on the first syllable of their root, but not after prefixes like be-, ge-, ver-, and borrowed words vary."
- `spelling.predictability: high` as briefed: true for reading aloud (the exceptions are loanwords and a few spellings like ie / ei); not for writing. `tones.system: none`.

**Grammar**
- **`word_order: flexible` [CHECK, imperfect fit].** German is V2 in main clauses (the verb is second whatever comes first), verb-final in subordinate clauses, and the two parts of a separable verb go to opposite ends. `svo` would say "like English" and hide the real difference; `flexible` ("because endings show who does what") is not wrong (the case endings on the articles do let the object come first) but it does not say the verb is fixed. The four examples show the main points: Ich sehe den Hund / Heute sehe ich den Hund (verb stays second) / Ich weiß, dass du den Hund siehst (verb goes to the end) / Ich rufe dich morgen an (separable verb). Suggest a new value `v2` with its own label if a reviewer agrees.
- `cases: some`, `count: 4` (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive). Examples: the declension of one noun, then the same two nouns swapped (Der Mann sieht den Hund / Den Mann sieht der Hund) so the learner sees that only the little words show who does the seeing. `noun_gender: three`, `values: masculine, feminine, neuter`, `articles: der, die, das`, six nouns (der Tisch, die Lampe, das Haus, der Hund, die Katze, das Buch).
- **`articles: definite_indefinite`**, `trait.formal_you: distinct` (`informal: du`, `formal: Sie`), `verb_endings: many` (`example_verb: lernen`, a regular verb on purpose, not sprechen with its vowel change), `subject_pronouns: required` (casual speech can drop ich: "Bin müde", but the standard sentence needs it), `adjective_position: before_noun` (with agreeing endings: ein großes Haus, eine rote Lampe), `questions: intonation_or_inversion` (the verb comes first: Sprichst du Deutsch?), `word_building: inflecting` (Kind / Kinder; compounds as the second and third examples: Handschuh, Krankenhaus). `classifiers: none`, `dual_number: none`.
- Compound nouns and separable verbs have no trait of their own in `slots.json`; they live in `word_building` and `word_order` examples. **[CHECK]** that is enough, or whether a trait `compounding` is wanted.
- The English glosses of the grammar examples add short remarks in brackets ("the verb stays in second place") that are facts about the sentence, not claims of verification.

**Overview**
- `family_id: indo_european`, `branch_ids: [germanic, west_germanic]` (matches the existing draft file in the real tree). Because the app's relation check treats a shared branch id as "same branch", Dutch, English and Afrikaans (if they list `west_germanic`) read as "close cousins" of German and a Scandinavian language that lists only `germanic` reads as the same branch too. **[CHECK]** that this is the intended reading for the other realizations.
- `related_language_tags`: `nl af en sv da nb`, all in the registry (Dutch and Afrikaans are the closest; English a more distant West Germanic cousin; Swedish, Danish, Norwegian are North Germanic). Frisian, Luxembourgish and Yiddish are not in the registry's lists.
- `origin_region_ids: [europe]`; `reach.regions: [europe, americas, africa]`. **[CHECK]**: only Europe has official status (Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Belgium, South Tyrol); Namibia recognises German as a national language and there are long-standing communities in the Americas (the USA, Brazil, Argentina, Chile, Paraguay). If "where it's spoken" should mean daily-life official use, use `[europe]`.
- Hook: `hook.target` "Guten Tag" (hello, good day), decoys "Danke" (thank you) and "Wasser" (water). "Hallo" was not used because it sounds like English. The formal / regional greetings (Grüß Gott, Moin, Servus) are not used.

## 3. Bridge (`bridge-en-de.json`)

- English and German are close cousins (both West Germanic), so the bridge is mostly **shared inherited words**, then four **English borrowings from German**. There is **one `helpful_transfer` note** (the app shows only the first note's text), 18 pairs; its text says honestly which kind each group is. A second note of kind `false_friend` has 12 pairs.
- Cognates (14): water/Wasser, hand/Hand, house/Haus, book/Buch, apple/Apfel, father/Vater, mother/Mutter, brother/Bruder, finger/Finger, winter/Winter, milk/Milch, salt/Salz, night/Nacht, friend/Freund. All are standard Germanic cognates; I read the en.wiktionary German etymology of each of the 14 (Wasser, Hand, Haus, Buch, Apfel, Vater, Mutter, Bruder, Finger, Winter, Milch, Salz, Nacht, Freund): each comes from a Proto-Germanic form that English shares (several entries name the English word as a cognate; Bruder, Milch and Nacht rest on the Proto-Germanic reconstruction and the standard English derivations, not on a named cognate). The sound shifts (t to z/ss, p to pf, k to ch, d to t, th to d) hide some of them. Words I considered and left out as too distant or with a meaning drift: Garten/garden (English from French), Hund/hound (English "hound" is a kind of dog), Kind/kid, Bett/bed was fine but "Bett" is used in a contrast pair.
- Borrowings into English (4): kindergarten, rucksack, angst, wanderlust (en.wiktionary's English etymology says each is borrowed from German; for angst it adds "or Danish", and the word was first written with a capital A in English); `to_meaning` is given where English narrows the sense (Kindergarten "children's garden, nursery school", Rucksack "backpack", Angst "fear", Wanderlust "the urge to travel").
- False friends (12): gift/Gift (poison), become/bekommen (to get, to receive), rat/Rat (advice, council; the animal is Ratte), chef/Chef (boss; the cook is Koch), handy/Handy (mobile phone), art/Art (kind, sort, way; art is Kunst), also/also (so, therefore; the English sense is auch), sensible/sensibel (sensitive; sensible is vernünftig), mist/Mist (manure, rubbish), brief/Brief (letter), bald/bald (soon), fast/fast (almost). Spellings and meanings are the textbook ones; **[CHECK]** each meaning with a native speaker (`Mist` also means "nonsense" in speech, `Rat` also "advice").

## 5. Things I noticed about the lesson that you may want to act on

1. A Latin-script reader gets only the short lesson (section 2), so the easy-first order of groups 1 to 6 is used only for readers of other scripts.
2. The `layer.latin.note` problem (shared across Latin-script languages).
3. The `upperAndLower` glyph for ß.
4. The mark id `diaeresis` for the umlaut.
5. The generic labels `trait.word_order.flexible` and `stress.unpredictable` are not very true for German; `v2` and `mostly_initial` values are suggested above.
