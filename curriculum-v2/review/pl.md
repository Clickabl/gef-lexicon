## A. Things that need a decision (read these first)

1. **Digraphs are not symbols.** `ch cz dz dź dż rz sz` are written with letters the reader already has, and Wikipedia says they get no special place in alphabetical order, so `symbols` is the 32 letters only. They are taught in the sounds stop (tricky sounds `sz` and `rz` and `ch`; the `sz` note also covers `cz` and `ż`; the `ś` note covers `ć ź ń` and the `si ci zi ni` rule) and not as tiles. `dz`, `dź` and `dż` have no card of their own. **No `layer.latin.note` was added**: the writing-stop note is read per layer id and `layer.latin` is shared by every Latin-script language, so a Polish-only sentence there would show for Spanish and English too (the Turkish author reached the same conclusion). A maintainer who wants the digraph sentence at the writing stop needs a per-language note slot. Suggested text: "Polish also writes some single sounds with two letters: ch, cz, dz, rz, sz."
2. **q, v and x are not in the alphabet.** Per Wikipedia they are "mostly found in foreign words" and sometimes counted (35 letters then). The 32 letters are listed, so for an English reader the script lesson is the short one: only `ą ć ę ł ń ó ś ź ż` (9 letters) are new, in teaching parts 6 and 7, followed by the four marks. The false-friend letters (`c h j w y`, part 5) are not in that short lesson, because an English reader already "has" the letters; they are taught in the sounds stop (tricky sounds `w`, `c`, `j`, `y`, `ch`) and in the full lesson for readers of other scripts.
3. **Nasal vowels are written as vowels, `ɔ̃` and `ɛ̃` (traditional analysis).** Wikipedia says recent analyses treat `ą ę` as an oral vowel plus a nasal consonant (kąt /kɔnt/, ręka /rɛŋka/, wąż /vɔŋʂ/, and word-final `ą` as a nasal diphthong [ɔw̃]); Wiktionary writes those realizations. I used the single nasal-vowel symbol everywhere (the letter's `sound`, `sounds.vowels`, the words: mąka ˈmɔ̃ka, ręka ˈrɛ̃ka, pączki ˈpɔ̃tʂki) so the letter, the sound card and the word agree, and so the "new sounds" comparison marks them new. Both symbols are a base letter plus U+0303; they are written the same way in every file. **[CHECK]** whether a nasal-consonant transcription is preferred.
4. **IPA conventions** (broad phonemic, standard Polish, from Wiktionary with these simplifications): tie bars and syllable dots removed (`ts dz tʂ dʐ tɕ dʑ`, as the English file writes `tʃ`); the hushing consonants written `ʂ ʐ tʂ dʐ` (Wiktionary's, the retroflex-ish values), not `ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ` (the alternative Wikipedia mentions), so the app marks them new for an English reader, which matches how a learner experiences them; `y` written `ɨ` (Wiktionary's headword transcription uses `ɘ`; Wikipedia and the Russian drop use `ɨ`); `ɡ` is U+0261; `ɲ` for ń; `x` for ch and h; `w` for ł and `v` for w; `i` after a consonant is written as Wiktionary does, `pj`, `kj` (pierogi, kiełbasa), and the examples avoid those spellings where possible; no stress mark on one-syllable words (dom, kot, koń, ćma, źle, cześć); voicing assimilation and final devoicing applied (babka ˈbapka, krew is said "kref" in the label); the `ź` in źle and the `rz` of rzeka are written `ʑ` and `ʐ`. `sounds.consonants` has 28 symbols (`ŋ` is an allophone of `n` and left out); `sounds.vowels` has `i ɨ ɛ a ɔ u ɛ̃ ɔ̃`. **[CHECK]** all word IPA against a Polish pronunciation dictionary.
5. **`stress.pattern` = `mostly_penultimate`**, not a "fixed" value: Polish stress is penultimate with real exceptions (some loanwords stressed on the antepenult, fizyka; the conditional endings; some numerals), so "mostly" is the honest value. The generic label ("Most words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable. The stress usually isn't written.") is true.
6. **`spelling.predictability` = `high`.** Reading is regular once the digraphs and the `i` rule are known. What is not predictable is which spelling a word uses where two spell one sound: `ó` or `u`, `ż` or `rz`, `h` or `ch`. The `ó`, `rz` and `ch` sound notes say so. **[CHECK]**.
7. **Grammar values with an imperfect fit** (closest value chosen):
   - `noun_gender` = `three` (masculine, feminine, neuter). In the plural Polish also separates masculine-personal ("virile") nouns from all others; the contract has no slot for it, so it is not shown. A `virile_plural` trait would carry it.
   - **Aspect** (verbs come in imperfective and perfective pairs: jeść / zjeść) is the thing a learner meets first in Polish verbs and has no slot. A `verb_aspect` trait (`none`/`pairs`) is suggested. Not shown.
   - `cases` = `some`, `count: 7` (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, locative, vocative). The generic `some` label is "nouns, articles and adjectives change form": Polish has no articles, otherwise true.
   - `adjective_position` = `before_noun`. It is the normal order (duży dom, nowa książka); fixed names and classifying adjectives come after (język polski, "the Polish language"), shown as the third example. `either` would be defensible. **[CHECK]**.
   - `questions` = `particle`: the question word `czy` starts a yes/no question and is very common, but optional; raising your voice alone also works. Both are shown as examples 1 and 2. `intonation_or_inversion` is also true; `particle` was chosen because it is the more distinctive fact for an English or Spanish reader. **[CHECK]**.
   - `word_order` = `flexible` (neutral SVO; the case endings let the object come first, shown in example 2).
   - `subject_pronouns` = `often_dropped` (Mówię po polsku, with the ending saying "I").
   - `formal_you` = `distinct`, `informal: ty`, `formal: pan / pani`. Polish formal address is a title used like a third-person noun ("Jak się pan ma?", literally "how does the gentleman do?"), not a separate pronoun; the generic label ("a polite “you” and a friendly “you”") still fits. The formal example is to a man; women are addressed with `pani`.
   - `verb_endings` = `many`, `example_verb: mówić`; the second example shows that the past tense changes for gender (byłem / byłam), which the contract's label does not mention.
   - `dual_number` = `none` (lost around the 15th century; relics only in paired nouns like oko, ręka, ucho). `classifiers`, `articles` = `none` (Wikipedia notes `ten` and `jeden` sometimes behave like articles; not claimed).
8. **`articles` qualifier for the gender check.** Polish has no articles, but the gender check and the validator need one word per noun and `articles` on `trait.noun_gender`. I used the possessive `mój / moja / moje` ("my"), as the Russian drop did for `мой / моя / моё`. I avoided `ten / ta / to` because `to` is also "this is". Six nouns, two of each gender: dom, książka, okno, kot, woda, morze. **[CHECK]** that `moje` for the neuter is not confused with the plural `moje` by the check.

## C. Conventions I chose (please confirm or change)

**The alphabet**
- One layer, `latin`, `alphabet`, `ltr`, `has_case: true`, `joined: false`, `vowels: all_written`, role `primary`. `marked` is empty: `ą ć ę ł ń ó ś ź ż` are separate letters of the alphabet, so they are `symbols`, not marked variants.
- `symbols`: the 32 letters in alphabet order, lowercase: a ą b c ć d e ę f g h i j k l ł m n ń o ó p r s ś t u w y z ź ż.
- **Seven teaching groups of 4 or 5**, easy first for an English reader: (1) `a m t k`, (2) `o e i u` the vowels, (3) `b d p f g`, (4) `l n s z r`, the letters that look and sound like English ones (r is a trill, g is always hard); (5) `c h j w y`, the false friends (c = ts, h = x, j = y, w = v, y = a new vowel); (6) `ó ł ą ę`, letters whose mark changes the sound of the base letter (ó = u, ł = w, the two nasal vowels); (7) `ś ć ź ń ż`, the soft and hushing consonants. So for an English reader (short lesson) the new letters fall into exactly parts 6 and 7, two sensible groups of 4 and 5. Every letter is in exactly one group, and no example word contains more group mates than the hear-and-pick can absorb (the most is one: mama has `a`, kot has `t`, ulica has `i`; the other 29 contain none). `ó` is taught with the nasal vowels, not next to `u` (to keep the English-reader short lesson in two clean groups); the `u` / `ó` sameness is in the sound card `ó`.
- **Examples.** One very common, concrete, short word (7 letters at most) for each of the 32 letters. Mostly native everyday words; four are old loans as common as any native word would be: `ananas`, `lampa`, `tort`, `herbata`. 26 of the 32 words start with their letter. The six that do not are ą (mąka), e (ser), ę (ręka), ń (koń), ó (góra) and y (syn): no native word starts with ą, ę, ń, ó or y in everyday Polish, and the only common concrete word starting with e is a loan (ekran). **[CHECK]** the weaker picks: `igła` (needle), `pole` (field; note that English "Pole" is a person from Poland), `fala` (wave), `tort` (a layered cake), `źle` (badly, the only abstract word; `ź` starts almost no concrete word, `źrebak` "foal" is rarer), `ulica`.
- `sound` is the letter's own main sound, broad IPA: a ɛ i ɔ u ɨ, ą ɔ̃, ę ɛ̃, c ts, ć tɕ, h x, j j, ł w, ń ɲ, ó u, r r (trill), s ɕ for ś, w v, ź ʑ, ż ʐ, g ɡ. `u` and `ó` both say `u`, by design.
- `name` is the letter's Polish name: a, a z ogonkiem, be, ce, cie, de, e, e z ogonkiem, ef, gie, ha, i, jot, ka, el, eł, em, en, eń, o, o z kreską, pe, er, es, eś, te, u, wu, igrek, zet, ziet, żet. Wikipedia and Wiktionary give `ą ę ó` as their names (`ą`, `ę`, `ó`) and `y` as `y` or `igrek`; I used the spelled-out forms Wiktionary lists as alternatives (`a z ogonkiem`, `e z ogonkiem`, `o z kreską`, `igrek`) so that the name question ("Which one is called ...?") is not a circular "Which one is called ą?" and so all 32 names are different. `ó` is also called `u kreskowane` and `u zamknięte`. **[CHECK]**.
- `looks_like` is set on `ą` (a), `ć` (c), `ę` (e), `ł` (l), `ń` (n), `ó` (o), `ś` (s), `ź` (z), `ż` (z): the new letters that wear a mark and are read as the base letter by mistake. It is **not** set on `c h j w y r`: they are the same Latin letters an English reader knows (the German drop did set it for j v w; the Turkish drop did not, for the reason that "Looks like c" on the tile for c is nonsense; I followed the Turkish drop). Their different sounds are in the tricky sounds.
- There is no `reading` (the script is Latin).
- **Marks.** Four, all separate letters as well, so the short lesson shows the same four letters twice (once as a letter in parts 6 and 7, once in the last group as the mark that makes them): `ogonek` (value ą, role `nasalization`, example mąka), `acute` (value ś, role `sound_change`, example ślimak; it stands for ć ń ś ź and ó), `stroke` (value ł, `sound_change`, example łopata), `dot_above` (value ż, `sound_change`, example żaba). They give the learner the marks' names (ogonek is Polish for "little tail"), which the letter tiles do not. A reader who finds the repetition unwelcome can delete the four `writing.latin.mark.*` facts and their four glosses and the three `mark.*` labels; nothing else depends on them. The Spanish and French readers already have `acute`, so the lesson skips it for them.
- **Punctuation.** `„` (the low opening quotation mark, Polish "cudzysłów"; the closing mark is `”`, in the same example „Cześć!”). No `sound` and no `reading`, per the new rule. **The dialogue dash is left out of `punctuation` on purpose:** Polish starts a line of dialogue with a long dash (the em dash, U+2014), and that character must not appear in any file. Say so if the lesson should teach it. Secondary quotes (`«»`) and the apostrophe (not Polish) are not listed.

**Sounds**
- Twelve `sounds.tricky.*`: nasal vowel (ą, mąka), ł (łopata), w (woda), c (cebula), j (jajko), sz (szafa), rz (rzeka), ś (ślimak), ch (mucha), y (dym), ó (góra), and a consonant cluster (pt, ptak). Rule followed from the Russian drop: no tricky word contains another tricky spelling, because the app's "which spelling makes the sound in this word?" question offers all spellings as options; the one exception is `mucha`, whose `ch` contains a `c` (which is also a card, /ts/). It cannot be avoided (every `ch` word has a `c`); the card is heard, not read, and /x/ is not /ts/. Two spellings that make one sound are never both cards (`rz` and not `ż`, `ó` and not `u`, `ch` and not `h`, `ś` and not `si`), otherwise the hear-and-pick would have two right answers.
- **Left out of the cards on purpose** (to keep the stop to six short pages): final devoicing (it is in the `w` note: krew is said "kref"), `ć` `ź` `ń` and `dz dź dż` (in the `ś` and `sz` notes), `cz`, and the trilled `r` (an English r is understood). **[CHECK]** whether a devoicing card should be added.
- **Four contrast pairs**, all real everyday words, glossed: kat / kąt (executioner / corner, a, nasal ɔ̃), stal / stał (steel / stood, l against ł), kasa / kasza (cash desk / groats, s against sz), był / bił (was / hit, y against i, and a clean parallel to the Russian был / бил). Rejected: `łaska / laska` (the second word has a vulgar colloquial sense in Wiktionary), `ława / lawa` (Wiktionary's entry for ława gives only dialect senses), `czy / ci` (two unstressed function words, hard to hear). **[CHECK]** that all eight words sound as written on the speech engine, especially the nasal in `kąt`. There is no stress pair (Polish stress does not tell words apart except in a few loan words, muzyka).
- The soft series (ś ć ź ń) against the hard (sz cz ż) has no contrast pair of its own, only the s / sz pair; a native speaker may want one (`czy / ci`, `kosz / koś`).
- `tones.system: none`.

**Overview**
- `family_id: indo_european`, `branch_ids: [slavic, west_slavic]`. Because the app's relation check treats a shared branch id as "same branch", a Slavic language that lists `slavic` will read as a close cousin to Polish; the Russian drop notes the same.
- `related_language_tags`: `cs sk hsb dsb` (the closest West Slavic relatives in the registry) then `uk ru` (East Slavic cousins), all in the registry. Not listed: `be bg sr hr bs sl mk`.
- `origin_region_ids: [europe]`; `reach.regions: [europe, americas]` (Europe, plus emigrant communities in the US, Canada, Argentina and Brazil per Wikipedia; Australia and Israel are named there too, so `oceania` and `asia` could be added). **[CHECK]**.
- Hook: `hook.target` cześć (/tʂɛɕtɕ/: informal "hi" and "bye"), decoys dziękuję (thank you) and tak (yes). The polite greeting "dzień dobry" was not used (two words, longer to hear). Wiktionary: cześć is used with people you call by first name; the gloss says "friendly, informal".

## D. Bridge (`bridge-en-pl.json`)

- English and Polish are distant cousins (both Indo-European), so the bridge is mostly **English words that came from Polish**, then **old shared words**. There is **one `helpful_transfer` note** (18 pairs; the app shows only the first note's text, so the one text says honestly which kind each group is), then one `false_friend` note (7 pairs).
- Loanwords (8): pierogi, kielbasa, zloty, babka, paczki, mazurka, horde, bigos. Checked in Wiktionary's English entries: pierogi (borrowed from Polish, plural of pieróg), kielbasa, zloty, paczki (plural of pączek), bigos (all "borrowed from Polish"); **babka** "from a Slavic language likely via Yiddish" (the search tool's summary of Merriam-Webster says Yiddish babke from Polish babka, and that English also took a second, Polish-style sense directly; **[CHECK]** the route wording); **mazurka** "via German and Russian, from Polish mazurek"; **horde** "from Middle French, from German, from Polish horda, from Russian, from a Turkic language" (a word that passed through Polish, not a Polish word). Not used: `polka` (Wiktionary: from Czech, not Polish), `vodka` (English from Russian), `robot` (Czech), `gherkin` (Dutch, "probably via Slavic"), `szlachta`, `sejm`, `krakowiak` (rare). `to_meaning` is set where the Polish word means more or something else (babka "grandmother; also a yeast cake", złoty "golden; the Polish currency", kiełbasa "sausage", pączki "filled doughnuts", horda "a horde; also a gang"). English "kielbasa" and "paczki" drop the `ł` and `ą`.
- Old shared words (10): brother/brat, sister/siostra, mother/matka, new/nowy, two/dwa, three/trzy, water/woda, mouse/mysz, nose/nos, wolf/wilk. Etymologies checked in Wiktionary (Polish entries; Proto-Slavic from Proto-Indo-European: brat *bʰréh₂tēr, siostra *swésōr, nowy *néwos, trzy *tréyes, woda *wódr̥, mysz *múHs, nos *néh₂s; matka is a Proto-Slavic diminutive of *màti from *méh₂tēr). **[CHECK]** `dwa` and `wilk` (standard Indo-European etymology; the wilk entry only says "inherited from Old Polish"). Words considered and left out: oko, imię, serce, mleko, jabłko, gęś, gość, wdowa, znać (true cognates too, but the English word is much harder to see).
- False friends (7, each with `to_meaning`): pension/pensja (salary), actual/aktualny (current), eventually/ewentualnie (possibly, if need be), sympathetic/sympatyczny (nice; Wiktionary lists "sympathetic" only as a medical sense), data/data (date), fabric/fabryka (factory), list/list (a letter). **[CHECK]** each with a native speaker (`sympatyczny` is the softest).
- Bridge IPA follows the realization conventions (A4).
