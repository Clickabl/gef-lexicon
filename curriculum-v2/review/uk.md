# Ukrainian (`uk`): needs, conventions and items to check

All content is `candidate`; nothing is verified. Items marked **[CHECK]** are where a native-speaker or linguist reviewer should look first.

How it was checked: the four realizations, the English locale additions and the bridge were overlaid on a scratch copy of the current `gef-lexicon` tree (including the live `ru` and `ja` realizations and the newest `scripts/validate-curriculum-v2.mjs`), and the validator passed with `uk` as `completeness: "complete"`, `slots_version: 2`. The real tree was not touched. I read the app's intro code (`introScript.ts`, `introSound.ts`, `introGrammar.ts`, `introShared.ts`, `introOverview.ts`, `introWriting.ts`) to see how the facts are used; where that changed a choice it is said below.

Sources: en.wiktionary Ukrainian pronunciation (the `{{uk-IPA}}` output, read through the MediaWiki API) for the stress and IPA of every example word; en.wikipedia "Ukrainian alphabet" (33 letters, names `йот` and `м'який знак`, straight apostrophe, not a letter), "Ukrainian phonology" (six vowels /i ɪ u ɛ ɔ a/, /ʋ/, /ɦ/, /ʃtʃ/, palatalization), "Romanization of Ukrainian" (the 2010 national table, Cabinet of Ministers Resolution 55); en.wiktionary etymology sections for borscht, hryvnia, Cossack, pysanka, bandura, hopak, vyshyvanka, varenyky, сестра, мати, три, ніс, вода; a search on Ukrainian null subjects (see `subject_pronouns`).

## 2. Conventions I chose (please confirm or change)

**Letters and groups**
- `symbols`: the 33 letters of the Ukrainian alphabet in alphabetical order, lowercase: `а б в г ґ д е є ж з и і ї й к л м н о п р с т у ф х ц ч ш щ ь ю я`. `ь`, `й`, `ї`, `є`, `ґ` are separate letters, so no diaeresis or breve marks exist. The apostrophe is punctuation, not a letter (`punctuation: ["'"]`).
- Nine teaching groups of 3 to 5, easy-first, following the brief: (1) `а е о` and (2) `к м т` look and sound like Latin; (3) `в н р` and (4) `с у х` are the look-alikes that sound different (`looks_like` B H P C y X); (5) `б д л п` and (6) `з ф г и` are new shapes (mostly familiar sounds; `г` and `и` are the two that mislead); (7) `ж ч ш ц щ` the hushing sounds; (8) `я ю й ь` the y-sounds and the soft sign; (9) `і ї є ґ`, the letters a Russian reader does not have (the short lesson for a Russian reader is exactly this group plus the apostrophe). Every letter is in exactly one group. In every group no example word contains another letter of the same group, so "which letter is in the word you hear?" never has two right answers.
- **Apostrophe.** Straight `'` (U+0027), the same character the lexicon's existing Ukrainian text uses and the one on keyboards. Unicode's recommended character is `ʼ` (U+02BC); **[CHECK]** whether the app's text-to-speech and any lookup want one or the other. The example is `м'яч` (ball). It has no `sound` or `reading` (punctuation is exempt in the validator and the app only shows what exists); the gloss says what it does: "keeps м and я apart".
- `looks_like` is set on в (B), н (H), р (P), с (C), у (y), х (X), п (n), ь (b) and ї (ï): the resemblances that mislead. Not set for з, ч, я, є, г, д, ю, which only resemble digits, Greek or a mirrored letter.
- **`reading` follows the 2010 national romanization (KMU), with deliberate departures [CHECK]:**
  - `г` is `h` and `ґ` is `g` (KMU). `х` `kh`, `ц` `ts`, `ч` `ch`, `ш` `sh`, `щ` `shch`, `ж` `zh`, `и` `y`, `і` `i`.
  - `є ї ю я` use the KMU **word-initial** forms (`ye yi yu ya`), because a letter on its own is a word-initial letter; KMU writes `ie i iu ia` elsewhere in a word.
  - **`й` is `j`, not KMU `y`/`i`.** KMU would give `й` the reading `y` (initial) or `i` (elsewhere), colliding with `и` (`y`) and `і` (`i`). The app's sound question and match drill break on duplicate readings (`soundQuestion` filters on them, `matchGlyphs` uses the reading as the answer), so all 33 readings are different. `j` is the same choice the Russian file made (ISO 9). An English reader may expect `y`; the IPA `/j/` is shown next to it.
  - **`ь` is `ʹ` (U+02B9), not a KMU value.** KMU leaves the soft sign out; the Wikipedia alphabet table and ISO 9 use `ʹ`. The tile reads "ʹ · /ʲ/". **[CHECK]** how it renders.
- `name` is "Cyrillic (transliteration)", as in the Greek and Arabic files: а (a), бе (be), ве (ve), ге (he), ґе (ge), де (de), е (e), є (ye), же (zhe), зе (ze), и (y), і (i), ї (yi), йот (yot), ка (ka), ел (el), ем (em), ен (en), о (o), пе (pe), ер (er), ес (es), те (te), у (u), еф (ef), ха (kha), це (tse), че (che), ша (sha), ща (shcha), м'який знак (miakyi znak), ю (yu), я (ya). **[CHECK]** all of them against the current orthography. Wikipedia's alphabet table gives `й` as "йот" and `ь` as "м'який знак" (an apostrophe inside the name); I could not confirm any older name such as "і кратка" from the sources I read.
- `sound` is the letter's own main sound, broad IPA: `a ɛ ɔ u i ɪ` for the vowels, `є ї ю я й` as `jɛ ji ju ja j`, `ь` as `ʲ` (the softness it marks, not a sound), `в` `ʋ`, `г` `ɦ`, `ґ` `ɡ` (U+0261), `х` `x`, `ц` `ts`, `ч` `tʃ`, `ж` `ʒ`, `ш` `ʃ`, `щ` `ʃtʃ`.
- Example words are concrete, short (7 letters at most; `будинок` is the longest), plain (no stress marks, as in real text) and native where a native word is as common. **Loans that were the best picture-able choice for their letter [CHECK]:** `акула`, `євро` (almost every word starting with `є` is a loan or an inflected form), `йогурт`, `фото`, `чай`. For `е` I used `мед` rather than a word starting with `е` (almost all of those are loans, and `екран` also contains `а`). `хата` is the traditional village house (`будинок` is the everyday "house"); `шапка` is a winter cap (hat in general is `капелюх`); `око` is slightly literary for "eye" (everyday plural is `очі`). The letters `и`, `у`, `е`, `і`, `ь` have an example that does not start with them: no everyday native word starts with `и` or `ь`, and the others (`у`, `е`, `і`) were chosen so that no word contains another letter of its group.
- Stress is marked in the IPA only; the example words are written without accent marks.

**Marks**
- Only one: `acute` (stress), `writing.cyrillic.mark.acute`, value `а́` (а + U+0301), example `замо́к` ("lock"; `за́мок` with the stress on the first syllable is "castle", checked in Wiktionary). It is also listed in `marked` as the ten stressed vowels `а́ е́ є́ и́ і́ ї́ о́ у́ ю́ я́`, which are not inventory letters and have no examples of their own. The acute is used only in dictionaries and learner books, never in ordinary text. A reader who already knows `acute` (a Russian or Spanish reader) skips it.

**Sounds**
- **IPA is broad and phonemic** (brief), from Wiktionary's `{{uk-IPA}}` simplified: the six vowels `i ɪ u ɛ ɔ a` (Wikipedia and Wiktionary write `a`, not `ɑ`; unstressed vowels keep their letter: no reduction shown, `о` stays `ɔ`), `ʋ` for `в` (it is `w` at the end of a syllable), `ɦ` for `г`, plain `l` for the dark `ɫ`, tie bars removed (`ts tʃ dz dʒ`). Softness is written `ʲ` only where the spelling shows it (`ь`, or `я ю є` after a consonant: `ˈdʲakuju`, `kinʲ`); the automatic softening of a consonant before `і` is not written, so `кіт` is `kit` (Wiktionary has `[kʲit]`). **[CHECK]** whether the app or the text-to-speech wants the softness before `і` written, and whether a narrower transcription would help.
- Eight `sounds.tricky.*`: `г` (гуска), `ґ` (ґанок), `и` (кит), `х` (хата, `sound.velar_fricative` reused), soft consonants (кінь, spelling `нь`), `щ` (щока), `р` (рука, `sound.trill` reused), `я` (яблуко). No tricky word contains another tricky spelling, because the app's "which spelling makes the sound in this word?" question offers all the spellings as options (this is why `г` is `гуска`, not `гора`, and `и` is `кит`, not `риба`).
- Four contrast pairs, all real and common: кит/кіт (`и` or `і`; whale or cat), ґрати/грати (`ґ` or `г`; bars or to play), стан/стань (hard or soft `н`; state or the command "stand/become!"), жити/шити (`ж` or `ш`; to live or to sew). **Deliberately left out:** the stress pair `за́мок`/`замо́к` (castle/lock), real and verified, because it is written the same without the accent and a text-to-speech voice may ignore the combining acute, so the listening question could not be answered from the page; the Russian file includes the same kind of pair with the same caveat. **[CHECK]** add it if audio for it is recorded.
- `stress.pattern: unpredictable` (brief): Ukrainian stress is free and mobile and ordinary text does not mark it.
- `spelling.predictability: high` **[CHECK].** Letters map to sounds very regularly (no vowel reduction written differently, no final devoicing, a few exceptions such as `легко` said with `х`), which is why `high`; but the unmarked, unpredictable stress means a learner cannot always say a word right from its spelling. `medium` is defensible. The two generic labels shown together ("a reliable guide to sound" and "stress changes from word to word and usually isn't written") do not contradict each other.
- `tones.system: none`. `sounds.vowels` lists six vowels; some descriptions write `/e/ /o/` instead of `/ɛ/ /ɔ/`.

**Grammar**
- **`articles` qualifier and the gender check [CHECK, imperfect fit].** Ukrainian has no articles (`trait.articles: none`), but the app's gender check ("Which little word goes with …?") and the validator need an `article` on every `noun.*` and `articles` on `trait.noun_gender`. I used the possessive `мій / моя / моє` ("my"), the word that agrees with the noun's gender (the same choice as the Russian file). Six nouns, two of each gender: стіл, друг (m), книга, кішка (f), вікно, місто (n).
- **`subject_pronouns: often_dropped` [CHECK].** Ukrainian verb endings carry the person, and a search of grammars and a recent study describe Ukrainian as partially null-subject, more so than Russian (past tense is the exception: it has no person endings). The alternative is `context_dependent`. A Russian reader will therefore see this as a difference from Russian (the Russian file says `required`).
- **`questions: particle` [CHECK].** A yes/no question can be made with the particle `чи` at the start ("Чи ти розмовляєш українською?"), or by intonation alone ("Це книга?"); both are shown. `intonation_or_inversion` is the closest alternative; I chose `particle` because `чи` is the structural thing an English or Russian reader does not have.
- **`cases: some`, `count: 7`** (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, locative, vocative). The vocative is alive (`мамо!`). The example lists six forms of мама; the locative is the same as the dative (мамі), so it is not listed twice.
- `formal_you: distinct` (ти / ви). `verb_endings: many`, example verb `говорю` (говорити). `word_order: flexible`, both orders shown. `word_building: inflecting`, examples `говоримо` and the gender-marked past `читав / читала`. `adjective_position: before_noun`. `noun_gender: three` (masculine, feminine, neuter). `classifiers: none`. `dual_number: none`: a few relics exist (очі, вуха; the nominative plural after 2 to 4) but there is no productive dual.

**Overview**
- `family_id: indo_european`, `branch_ids: [slavic, east_slavic]`; `related_language_tags`: `be`, `ru`, `pl`, `sk`, `cs` (all in the registry; Belarusian and Russian are the East Slavic relatives, the others are West Slavic cousins). Because the app's relation check treats any shared branch id as "same branch", Ukrainian and Russian read as close cousins (they are both East Slavic), and Polish or Czech as the "Slavic" level only if their files list `slavic` and not `east_slavic`.
- `reach.regions: [europe]` (brief); the diaspora in the Americas is large but not listed. `origin_region_ids: [europe]`.
- Hook: `hook.target` привіт ("hi", informal), decoys дякую ("thank you") and так ("yes"). They carry `reading` as the Chinese and Russian files do for their scripts; the readings are KMU (`pryvit`, `diakuiu`, `tak`). **[CHECK]** `diakuiu` (KMU for дякую) reads oddly to an English eye ("dyakuyu"); the brief asked for KMU, so I kept it. The app does not show the hook `reading` today.

## 3. Bridge (`bridge-en-uk.json`)

- English and Ukrainian are distant cousins, so there is **one `helpful_transfer` note** (the app shows only the first note's text as the page text, and puts the pairs of all `helpful_transfer` notes under it), whose text says honestly which kind each group is: first English words that came from Ukrainian (8), then old shared words (10), which are true relatives and not borrowings. 18 pairs.
- **Borrowed words, verified in en.wiktionary etymology sections:** borscht ("From Yiddish באָרשט, from Russian or Ukrainian борщ": the Ukrainian source is shared with Russian; **[CHECK]** the wording), Cossack (from Middle French cosaque, from Middle Polish Kozak, from Middle Ukrainian козакъ; the word is ultimately Turkic), hryvnia (from Ukrainian), pysanka (from Ukrainian), bandura (borrowed from Ukrainian, "possibly through Italian and Polish"), hopak (from Ukrainian), vyshyvanka (from Ukrainian), varenyky (from Ukrainian, plural). Left out: hetman (English via Polish, from German), dumka (real but obscure), perogy (an English spelling variant of pierogi; Polish route). `to_ipa` is phonemic; `to_meaning` is added where the Ukrainian word means something the English reader will not guess (the app does not show `to_meaning` on helpful-transfer cards, so it is informational).
- **Old shared words:** брат, сестра, мати, два, три, ніч, ніс, вода, вовк, вдова. Inherited from Proto-Indo-European through Proto-Slavic, as English brother, sister, mother, two, three, night, nose, water, wolf, widow are through Proto-Germanic. Checked in the Ukrainian Wiktionary etymologies for сестра (PIE *swésōr), мати (*méh₂tēr), три (*tréyes), ніс (*néh₂s), вода (*wódr̥); **[CHECK]** брат, два, ніч, вовк, вдова from standard Indo-European etymology (not displayed by the pages I read). Words I considered and left out as too distant to recognise: мед (cognate of mead, but the Ukrainian means honey), молоко, сніг, серце, ім'я.
- **One `false_friend` note, 5 pairs:** magazine/магазин (shop, store), actual/актуальний (current, relevant, up to date), fabric/фабрика (factory), prospect/проспект (avenue), baton/батон (a loaf of bread). Meanings checked in Wiktionary (магазин, актуальний, фабрика, проспект, батон). **[CHECK]** with a native speaker: `проспект` also means a prospectus or brochure; `акуратний` ("careful, precise, neat") was dropped as accurate/акуратний overlaps too much; "decade/декада" (ten days) was dropped because I could not check it in a Ukrainian entry.
